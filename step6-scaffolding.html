Scaffolding: Support Without Dilution

Scaffolding refers to the temporary supports—the "bridges"—that help students reach challenging learning. The key word is temporary. Scaffolds are there to help students access challenge, not to remove the challenge entirely.

The goal: Goals are never diluted. All students work toward the same KUDs. But some students need more support to get there, and scaffolding provides that support.

Core Principles of Effective Scaffolding

1. Scaffolds Are Temporary

Like physical scaffolding on a building, instructional scaffolds should be removed once they're no longer needed. The goal is student independence, not permanent dependence on supports.

2. Scaffolds Support Access, Not Reduction

Scaffolds help students access challenging content—they don't make content less rigorous. All students should be grappling with the same essential understandings.

3. Scaffolds Are Matched to Need

Different students need different scaffolds. One student might need a graphic organiser; another might need a thinking partner. Assess what's blocking access and scaffold specifically for that barrier.

4. Scaffolds Should Fade

Plan for the gradual removal of scaffolds. Students should become less reliant on supports over time as they develop skills and confidence.

Types of Scaffolds and When to Use Them

2

Graphic Organizers

▼

Visual frameworks that help students organise thinking, see relationships between ideas, or structure their work. Particularly helpful for students who struggle with working memory or organisation.

When to use: When students understand concepts but struggle to organise or connect ideas. When tasks require holding multiple pieces of information in mind simultaneously.
Common types: Venn diagrams, T-charts, mind maps, flowcharts, story maps, cause-effect diagrams, KWL charts

AI Prompts:

Create a Graphic Organizer

Students need to [describe task, e.g., "compare two characters" or "organise research notes"].

Design a graphic organiser that:

  • Provides visual structure for this thinking
  • Shows relationships between ideas
  • Includes prompts or labels to guide student thinking
  • Can be completed by hand or digitally

Describe the layout so I can create it for students.

Fade a Graphic Organizer

I've been using this graphic organiser: [describe organiser]. Students are getting better at [skill]. Design three versions that progressively fade the support:

  • Version 1 (Full support): All sections labeled, prompts provided
  • Version 2 (Partial support): Main structure present but fewer prompts
  • Version 3 (Minimal support): Basic framework only, students generate details
3

Sentence Starters & Thinking Stems

▼

Phrases that help students begin their thinking, writing, or discussion. Particularly helpful for students who know what they want to say but struggle to start, or who need models for academic language.

When to use: For writing tasks, class discussions, or any time students need to articulate thinking in academic language. Especially helpful for EALD students.
Examples: "The evidence suggests that..." "In contrast to..." "One possible explanation is..." "This connects to... because..."

AI Prompts:

Generate Sentence Starters

Students need to [describe thinking/writing task]. Create 8-10 sentence starters that would help them:

  • Begin their response
  • Add evidence or examples
  • Make connections
  • Draw conclusions

Use academic but accessible language appropriate for [grade level].

4

Chunking & Step-by-Step Instructions

▼

Breaking complex tasks into smaller, manageable steps. Particularly helpful for students who feel overwhelmed by large tasks or have executive functioning challenges.

When to use: When students understand the content but struggle with multi-step processes or feel paralyzed by complex assignments.
Example: Instead of "Write an essay about X," provide: "Step 1: Read sources and take notes. Step 2: Organise notes into categories. Step 3: Write topic sentences. Step 4: Add evidence. Step 5: Write introduction and conclusion."

AI Prompts:

Break Down a Complex Task

This is the task I'm asking students to complete: [describe task]

Break it down into 5-8 clear, manageable steps that:

  • Are sequenced logically
  • Each feel achievable on their own
  • Include success criteria for each step
  • Build toward the final product

Use student-friendly language for [grade level].

Create a Checklist

Turn this multi-step task into a student-friendly checklist: [describe task]

Format as checkboxes so students can track progress. Include:

  • Clear action items
  • What "done" looks like for each item
  • Estimated time for each step
5

Models & Exemplars

▼

Showing students what quality work looks like. Models provide concrete examples of expectations rather than abstract descriptions.

When to use: When introducing new formats, genres, or quality standards. When students ask "What do you mean?" about expectations.
Best practices: Show multiple examples (not just one), analyse what makes them strong, provide both strong and developing examples for comparison

AI Prompts:

Create a Model

Create a model example of [type of work] that demonstrates the KUDs: [paste KUDs]

This will be shown to students as an example. Make it:

  • Grade-appropriate in language and complexity
  • Strong but not perfect (so students see it as achievable)
  • Clear in demonstrating the success criteria

Also provide 3-4 discussion points I can use when analysing this model with students.

Create Comparison Examples

Create two examples of [type of work]:

  • Example A: Strong example that meets all criteria
  • Example B: Developing example with clear areas for improvement

I'll use these with students to discuss what makes work strong. Provide questions I can ask to guide analysis.

6

Thinking Partners & Collaboration

▼

Pairing students strategically so they can support each other's thinking. The right partnership can scaffold learning through dialogue and shared problem-solving.

When to use: When students would benefit from talking through ideas, when multiple perspectives would deepen understanding, or when peer explanation could clarify confusion.
Structures: Think-pair-share, peer review, reciprocal teaching, jigsaw, collaborative problem-solving

AI Prompts:

Design Partner Work Structure

Students are working on [task]. Design a partner work structure that:

  • Gives both students clear roles
  • Ensures both are intellectually engaged (not one doing all the work)
  • Builds in accountability
  • Includes prompts for productive dialogue

How should partners be formed for this task (similar ability, mixed ability, shared interest)?

7

Worked Examples & Think-Alouds

▼

Demonstrating the thinking process, not just the final answer. Show students how an expert approaches the task, including false starts and self-correction.

When to use: When introducing new skills or problem types. When students know what to do but not how to think through it.
Key elements: Verbalize your thinking, show decision-making, explain why you're making certain choices, demonstrate what to do when stuck

AI Prompts:

Create a Think-Aloud Script

Write a think-aloud script for demonstrating [skill/process]. The script should:

  • Model the cognitive process, not just the mechanics
  • Include decision points ("I'm choosing to... because...")
  • Show what to do when confused or stuck
  • Be appropriate for [grade level] students

I'll use this to model the process before students try it themselves.

8

Academic Language & Vocabulary Scaffolds

▼

Provide explicit support for academic vocabulary through student-friendly definitions, organised word walls, visual supports, and opportunities for practice. These scaffolds help all students—especially EALD learners—access content-heavy material and develop academic language proficiency.

When to use: When content involves Tier 2 (high-utility academic words) or Tier 3 (domain-specific terminology), when students need to understand and use specific vocabulary, or when supporting EALD students.
Example in practice: A concept-categorised word wall for a science unit where terms are grouped by themes (e.g., "Forces," "Energy," "Motion") rather than alphabetically, with each term accompanied by a simple icon and student-friendly definition.

AI Prompts:

Create Tier 2/3 Vocabulary List with Definitions

Context: I'm teaching [subject/topic] to students in [Year Level].

Objective: Identify and define key academic vocabulary that students will need to understand this content.

Instructional nuance: Focus on Tier 2 (high-utility academic words like "analyse," "synthesise") and Tier 3 (domain-specific terms). Provide student-friendly definitions that are age-appropriate and avoid circular language.


Prompt:

Here is the content I'm teaching: [Insert content/text/lesson description]

Please:

  • Identify 8-12 key vocabulary terms (mix of Tier 2 and Tier 3)
  • For each term, provide:
    • A student-friendly definition (appropriate for [Year Level])
    • An example sentence showing the word in context
    • Note whether it's Tier 2 or Tier 3

Format the output as a table.

Design Concept-Categorised Word Wall

Context: I want to create a word wall that helps students see relationships between vocabulary terms rather than just an alphabetical list.

Objective: Organise vocabulary into meaningful concept categories to support memory and deeper understanding.

Instructional nuance: Group words by underlying concepts, themes, or processes—not just alphabetically.


Prompt:

Here are the key vocabulary terms for my [subject/topic] unit: [list terms]

Please organise these into 3-5 concept categories that would help students:

  • See connections between related words
  • Understand the 'big ideas' these words represent
  • Build mental schemas

For each category:

  • Provide a clear category name
  • List the terms that belong in it
  • Write a 1-sentence explanation of why these words go together

Add Visual Supports to Vocabulary

Context: I have key vocabulary that would be more accessible if students had visual cues alongside the definitions.

Objective: Suggest simple icons, symbols, or visual representations that would support vocabulary learning.

Instructional nuance: Visuals should be simple, unambiguous, and culturally appropriate. They're memory aids, not artwork.


Prompt:

Here are the vocabulary terms I need to teach: [list terms with definitions]

For each term, suggest:

  • A simple visual representation (icon, symbol, diagram, or gesture)
  • Why this visual support helps students remember/understand the term
  • How I could physically represent this in the classroom (e.g., printed icon, hand gesture, quick sketch)

Prioritise visuals that are:

  • Simple to draw or find
  • Unambiguous
  • Memorable

Generate Cloze Passage for Vocabulary Reinforcement

Context: Students have been introduced to key vocabulary and I want them to practice using it in context.

Objective: Create a cloze (fill-in-the-blank) passage that reinforces vocabulary in a meaningful way.

Instructional nuance: The passage should make sense and be engaging, not just a list of sentences. Blanks should require genuine thinking about word meaning.


Prompt:

I'm teaching [topic] to [Year Level] and these are the key vocabulary terms: [list terms]

Please create a short passage (150-200 words) where students fill in the blanks with these vocabulary words.

Requirements:

  • The passage should tell a coherent story or explain a concept related to [topic]
  • Each blank should have 2-3 plausible options (provide a word bank)
  • Include 8-10 blanks
  • Make it engaging and age-appropriate

Format:

  • The cloze passage with numbered blanks
  • Word bank (include 2-3 extra distractor words)
  • Answer key

What are likely barriers to success?

I'm teaching [topic/skill]. Students will be working on [describe task/assignment]. Based on what I know about my students: [describe readiness, common struggles]

Help me plan scaffolds by answering:

  • What are likely barriers to success? What might block students from accessing this challenge?
  • What scaffolds would address each barrier? Be specific about type and structure.
  • Who needs which scaffolds? Not everyone needs every scaffold.
  • How will I fade these scaffolds? What's the plan for removing them as students gain skill?
